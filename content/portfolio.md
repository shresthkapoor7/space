## writing

### finding where to start your research

_essay · 2026 · 6 min_

finding where to start your research is a hard problem. what is even harder is knowing what the next progression should be.

![Research desk illustration](https://i.redd.it/np8semrnlmt41.png)

as i started my final semester in the computer science master's program at new york university, we were tasked with writing an academic-level research paper for a class. the topic ai for games was completely new to me, and i had no idea where to begin. i made a rookie move: i went to chatgpt and asked, "tell me an ai for games research topic."

it spat out the voyager paper an llm-powered embodied lifelong learning agent in minecraft. it didn't fully click for me, but i figured, ***what if i did what voyager did, but swapped in reasoning models?*** i submitted the idea for my first assignment.

i got back a 7/10. the feedback noted a lack of creativity and no reasonable outcome.

that failure is why i started building sediment.

i wanted to solve two core problems:

1. **visualizing lineage:** knowing what should come next requires a deep understanding of what came before. i needed a workspace where you could visualize papers and actually understand their chronological progression.
2. **discovering the right topics:** finding the true map of the territory before diving in.

the vision for sediment became an agentic graphrag workspace where papers are laid out chronologically. more importantly, instead of just a standard summarizer tool, sediment features an ai agent that actually reads and analyzes those papers, allowing you to ask highly relevant, contextual questions.

but this brings me to the problem i am currently stuck on: discovering the right topics.

how do i leverage ai to create a visual map of subtopics? in ai for games, you have reinforcement learning, search and planning, procedural content generation, and so much more. papers rarely fit into just one category; they belong to multiple subtopics, creating a massive many-to-many relationship.

you can use a standard obsidian-like knowledge graph, but once you map multiple subtopics to hundreds of papers, it quickly turns into an unreadable hairball.
